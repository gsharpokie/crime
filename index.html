Is Crime Up?

Every year, Gallup asks Americans whether there’s more crime in the country than there was a year ago. Before you see what they said, and what actually happened, make three guesses.

The fuller picture

Crime fell a lot, but not every year. The violent victimization rate dropped about 71% from 1993 to 2024. It also bounced around: it rose from 2021 to 2022, and homicides jumped nearly 30% in 2020, the largest one-year increase the FBI has recorded.

The latest numbers are the lowest in decades. In 2025, the FBI reported a murder rate of 4.1 per 100,000 people, tied with 1955 and 1956 for the lowest since national tracking began in 1900. Violent crime overall fell 9.3% from 2024. Preliminary survey estimates from the Bureau of Justice Statistics also show serious violent victimization dropping 25% in 2025.

Perceptions caught up a little. In 2025, 49% of Americans said there was more crime in the U.S. than a year earlier, the lowest share since 2001. Republicans’ views changed most: 90% said crime was rising in 2024, compared with 54% in 2025.

Talk about it

  1. Gallup has found that most Americans say crime is rising in almost every year it has asked, including years when crime fell sharply. Where do people get their sense of how much crime there is?
  2. Why might people say crime is rising in the country but not in their own area? What do they know about their own area that they don’t know about the country?
  3. Psychologists call our tendency to judge how common something is by how easily examples come to mind the “availability heuristic.” How might news coverage and social media affect that?
  4. Who benefits when people believe crime is rising? Think about politicians, media outlets, and businesses that sell security products.
  5. The victimization survey asks people directly whether they were victims, including crimes never reported to police. Why might that give a different picture than police reports?

About the data

What people said: Gallup’s annual Crime survey, which asks “Is there more crime in the U.S. than there was a year ago, or less?” (since 1989) and the same question about “your area.” Gallup didn’t ask every year, so the lines skip some years. The 2025 survey was conducted by phone October 1–16, 2025, with 1,000 U.S. adults (margin of error ±4 points).

What happened: the Bureau of Justice Statistics’ National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS), which interviews about 240,000 people a year and counts violent crimes against people 12 and older, whether or not they were reported to police. It covers rape or sexual assault, robbery, aggravated assault, and simple assault, but not homicide, since victims can’t be interviewed. BJS says 2006 shouldn’t be compared with other years, so it’s left out. BJS redesigned the survey starting in 2024; the 2025 estimates come from the new version and aren’t comparable to this line, so the chart ends in 2024.
